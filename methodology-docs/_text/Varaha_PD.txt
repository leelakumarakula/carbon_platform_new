1.1 Summary Description of the Project

          The project intends to generate additional income to the small holder farmers of Indo- Gangetic
          plain region for adopting improved agricultural practices such as zero till farming, cover cropping,
          crop diversification, farm residue incorporation, optimizing fertilizer application and efficient
          water management which provide a potential to not just prevent emissions into the atmosphere
          but also create carbon sinks through sequestration of carbon back into the soil. Globally,
          agriculture and allied, account for almost 22% of the total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and
          hence is considered a major problem contributing to climate change1.
          In 2018, global emissions due to agriculture (within the farm gate and including related land
          use/land use change) were 9.3 billion tonnes of CO2 equivalent (CO2eq). Out of this, India is the
          third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases and accounted for about 650 Mt CO2 eq. of the total
          global emissions (FAO 2018). These high emissions are largely attributed to conventional
          practices such as flooded, transplanting method of cultivation in rice, multiple tillage operations,
          residue burning, flooding, monocropping, excessive usage of fertilizers and water, and absence
          of cover crops for Carbon (C) and Nitrogen (N) management in soils. The average Soil Organic
          Carbon (SOC) content in these soils is around 0.2­0.4% (MoA & FW 2019) as against the ideal
          (Bhattacharya, 2007).
          In November 2021, India announced its new climate targets at COP26, Glasgow under its
          `Panchamrit (or a 5 elixir) strategy'. Of these five strategies, three focused explicitly on reducing
          Carbon emissions.2 In order to curb the current extent of agricultural GHG emissions (national
          level  14%) and achieve these ambitious goals, business-as-usual approach would not suffice,
          and India must intensively strategize towards development of low-emission agriculture pathways.
          Despite its appeal, regenerative agriculture will take a long time to scale across Indian
          geographies especially because there is a lack of incentive to invest in regenerative practices
          with a long-term return on investment. The newly emerging carbon market for agriculture is
          envisioned to stimulate low-emission agricultural technologies. However, the transition to
          negative carbon agriculture requires a strong value proposition.
          In this context, the project implements faster adoption of improved agricultural practices by
          Indian farmers that can be the only solution to mitigate the GHG emissions, reverse land
          degradation and enable carbon sequestration, along with improving overall soil health and crop
          productivity. The benefits of regenerative agriculture to soil fertility are proven through extensive

1.2 Sectoral Scope and Project Type

          The sectoral scope of the project is VCS scope 14 "Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use"
          (AFOLU) and under the category Agriculture Land Management (ALM). The project constitutes
          activities that reduce as well as remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. It falls under the
          category of grouped project and is using the VCS methodology: "VM0042, Ver 1.0, Methodology
          for Improved Agricultural Land Management ".

1.3 Project Eligibility

          The project will implement sustainable improved agricultural practices. The project is eligible
          under the scope of the VCS Program. The grouped project is eligible as it fulfills the rules and
          requirements of the VCS Program criteria for GHG projects and programs.

          The project is being submitted under the VM0042 methodology for Improved Agricultural Land
          Management Version 1.0.

          The following conditions describe the compliance with the applicability conditions for the use of
          the VCS methodology VM0042 methodology for Improved Agricultural Land Management Version
          1.0.-

          a) Project activities are implemented on land that is either cropland or grassland at the project
               start date and remains cropland or grassland throughout the project crediting period (i.e.,
               land use change is not eligible, including conversion from cropland to grassland and
               grassland to cropland).

          b) The project area has not been cleared of native ecosystems within the 10-year period prior
               to the project start date.

          c) The project activity is not expected to result in a sustained reduction greater than 5% in

               the region or a comparable region.

          d) The project activity does not involve the application of biochar.
          e) The project activity does not occur on a wetland, however on crops subject to artificial

               flooding where it is proven that crop cultivation does not impact the hydrology of any nearby
               wetlands.

          All the activities being promoted in this project were shown in peer-reviewed scientific studies to
          successfully sequester soil organic carbon and mitigate gaseous emissions resulting from
          adoption of improved agricultural management practices included in the project description.

1.11 Description of the Project Activity

          The project is not located within a jurisdiction covered by a jurisdictional REDD+ program. The
          key objective of the project is to enable mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions by implementing
          regenerative agriculture practices. Developing farm-level adoption of climate-resilient production
          systems for rice, wheat, maize, cotton, mustard and sugarcane crops in the project area is critical
          for empowering small holder farmers in the region to sustain their livelihood and ensure income
          security.

          Smallholder agriculture is hindered by several inter-dependent factors such as low soil fertility,
          frequent dry spells, drought (climate change) and unsustainable agri-management practices.
          Ongoing and traditional agricultural practices, such as continuous monocropping, excessive use
          of synthetic fertilizers beyond the recommended dosage, poor soil, crop residue and irrigation
          management in the project locations have deteriorated soil productivity to the extent that many

conservation agriculture, such as minimum mechanical soil disturbance, permanent soil cover,

·         Minimum/Zero tillage [ZT]/Reduced Tillage [RT]

Zero tillage (ZT) implies planting crops in previously unprepared soil and is mainly considered a

farm management system. It is also known as zero till, no till or direct planting. The modern

concept of ZT tends to imply seeding a crop mechanically in undisturbed soil-covered plant

residues (Ekboir 2002). Continuous rice-wheat-maize cropping systems cause build-up of pests

and diseases. Reducing soil movement through ZT serves as an effective control measure for

pests (Malik et al. 2002). Rice-wheat-maize systems also cause land degradation, with excessive

groundwater pumping. Rice-wheat systems need to enhance their cost competitiveness in the

context of trade liberalization. Zero tillage potentially enables savings in energy (electricity, fuel),

water, labor, and other inputs such as use of machinery, costs of tillage operation, labor while

greatly conserving soil moisture. The advantages of ZT technology are thus manifold generating

higher yields at lower production costs and, being an environment- friendly practice that saves

water and soil (Hobbs et al. 1997).

·  Direct Seeded Rice (DSR)

Direct Seeded Rice (DSR) systems have significant potential to reduce the environmental

footprint and land degradation (Yadav et al., 2020). DSR is a suitable alternative to advance

climate-resilient methods in an efficient manner. The DSR option has the advantages of direct

seeding in low residue pressure, using the zero-tillage and multi-crop planter, and in high residue

pressure, with the Happy Seeder and Super Seeder. Furthermore, the DSR option has been

known to sustain soil systems and increase productivity and profitability (Samal et al., 2017). In

addition, adoption of the DSR option advances seeding by 15 days (Rao et al., 2021).

·  Crop residue management

The project activities aim to eliminate crop residue burning, as harvesting of various crops

generates large volume of residues both on and off farm. The generation of crop residues is

highest in Uttar Pradesh (60 Mt), followed by Punjab (51 Mt) and Maharashtra (46 Mt). Among

different crops, cereals generate maximum residues (352 Mt), followed by fibres (66 Mt), oilseeds

(29 Mt), pulses (13 Mt) and sugarcane (12 Mt). Cereal crops (rice, wheat, maize, millets)

contribute 70%, while rice crop alone contributes 34% to the crop residues. Sugarcane residues

consisting of top, and leaves generate 12 Mt, i.e., 2% of the crop residues in India.

In the project locations, a large portion of crop residue (about 116 MT) (Porichha et. al., 2021) is
recorded to be burnt `on-farm' primarily to clean the field for sowing the next crop. The problem
of `on-farm' burning of crop residues is intensifying in recent years due to shortage of human
labour, high cost of removing the crop residue from the field and mechanized harvesting of crops.
As per available estimates, burning of crop residues is predominant in four states, namely,
Haryana, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh & West Bengal.

In the current project, farmers who have now stopped burning are being enrolled for carbon
revenues because of preventing the CO2 and CH4 emissions because of burning activity.
Preventing burning itself would aid in SOC increases as the Carbon losses through burning will
be prevented. The crop residues could also be subjected to aerobic/vermicomposting or added
as mulch to improve SOC in the soils. Alternatively, the recently popularized farm machinery such
as Happy Seeders and Super Seeders could be used for direct incorporation of residues.

·  Farm mechanization

The Happy Seeder is a tractor-mounted machine that cuts and lifts rice straw, sows' wheat into

the soil, and deposits the straw over the sown area as mulch. Super Seeder is another farm

machinery which is considered as a single-pass solution to fulfil current farming needs to stop

crop residue burning. It can be used to plough paddy stubble back into soil and sow wheat seed

simultaneously in a single operation after the harvesting of the paddy. This enhances soil fertility

and carbon sequestration.

Hydrology: The Indo-Gangetic Plain (IGP) is divided into two drainage basins: the Punjab and

The Indo Gangetic Plain (IGP) is one of the world's most densely populated locations, owing to its
agriculturally productive soil. In terms of vegetation, the IGP is a reasonably similar biological
zone, however it has been separated into five broad transects based on physiography and bio-
climate (Narang and Virmani 2001). The Trans-Gangetic Plains (transects 1 and 2) cover huge
portions of Pakistan and India, including Punjab and Haryana. The areas in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar,
and Nepal are covered by Transects 3 and 4. Transect 5 includes the lower Gangetic Plains in

Soil Health: Intensive use of irrigation water in rice led to increased salinity. In the short term,
salinity built up leads to reduced yields, whereas, in the long term, it can lead to abandoning of
crop lands. Also, the heat from burning paddy straw penetrates 1 cm into the soil, elevating
the temperature to 33.8 to 42.2 degree Celsius. This kills the bacterial and fungal populations
critical for a fertile soil. Burning also causes damage to other micro-organisms as well as its
organic quality. One tonne of stubble burning leads to a loss of 5.5 kg N, 2.3 kg P, 25 kg K
and more than 1kg of Sulphur. Therefore, conventional agricultural practices and crop burning
practices are proving to be detrimental to soil health, crop productivity and farmer incomes in the
IGP region.

3.4 Baseline Scenario

          As per VCS VM0042 methodology, the most plausible baseline scenario is that each farmer
          continues to manage their field with the same conventional practices they were using before the
          implementation of the project activities. It is distinct for each farmer and sample unit. Typically, the
          baseline scenario for project instances includes conventional tillage practices that disturb soil,
          leaving minimal residue on the soil surface.
          The baseline scenario is formally defined by developing a schedule of activities existing prior to
          the project implementation for a period of 3 years or longer. Using historical management
          information from a sampling unit, the project proponent will establish the baseline schedule of
          activities. This will be the basis of modelling changes in soil organic carbon stocks in the dynamic
          baseline ex post following VM0042's Quantification Approach 1. Historical management
          activities also define fertilizer, fossil fuel combustion, and cropping activities resulting in N2O,
          CH4, and/or CO2 emissions, which are calculated for the baseline scenario using emission
          factors and rates or values derived from historical management information under Quantification
          Approach 3.

          Technological Barriers: Farmers are practising common conventional/ traditional practices
          since very long. They have limited technical knowledge related to the reduced fertiliser intake,
          reduction in tillage, improvement in crop residue, soil testing and soil nutrient management etc.
          Therefore, farmers were not able to change the traditional practices. Project Proponent through
          the project partners will impart training to the farmers regarding the regenerative agricultural
          practices to make them aware and will provide continuous technical support during the project
          lifetime. Hence, without the interventions of the project proponent the practices would not have
          been changed.

          Financial Barriers: There are a number of schemes and subsidies in India, for farmers adopting
          different practices, or for the equipment's used. But these funds are either insufficient15 or the
          farmer is unable to get the money on time in order to execute the practices in sustainable and
          timely manner. Moreover, farmers are unable to buy the expensive machineries and
          equipment's and hence they have not changed their traditional practices. Revenue generated
          from the sale of carbon credits along with overall increment in crop in productivity will overcome
          the financial barriers due to the higher costs and a lack of suitable incentives.

          Cultural and Social Barriers: There are a number of beliefs associated with different practices,
          like burning of crop residue helps control pests and diseases, tillage improves soil productivity
          etc. In addition, a large number of farmers have poor literacy rate having limited access to
          networks connecting them with higher level of governance and agricultural experts. Due to the
          implementation of the project, technicians and experts will visit the farms regularly to help
          farmers in order to adapt the regenerative agricultural practices.

          scaling up sustainable agriculture would be critical to improve farm incomes and bolster India's
          nutrition security in a climate-constrained future. Most conservation agriculture practices are
          being adopted by less than five million (or four per cent) of all Indian farmers and many are
